# Words for the Autumn Wind

On 11 July 2018, NFL.com published 149 words about a tryout. The Cleveland
Browns had spent a day looking at a defensive back from the Canadian league, a
23-year-old who had washed through the Edmonton Eskimos and an arena team in
Ohio and would never play a down in the NFL. The item exists because a team
worked out a player and somebody has to write that down. It is the lowest form
in the trade — no argument, no access, no reader who is not already lost.

The last fact in it calls him *a 5-foot-10, 190-pound cover man*.

Not a defensive back. The headline says defensive back. The league's own style
says defensive back, or DB, and the file next to this one on the same website
that afternoon almost certainly said one or the other. But in the sentence
carrying the only original detail in the piece — the height, the weight, the
undrafted college — the writer reached past the available word and took a
different one. Nobody was going to notice. That, I think, is the whole point.

The writer was Marc Sessler, and he did this for more than a decade. A backup
quarterback in a March roster item will probably not survive to September; he is
*looking for work come autumn*. A coach's praise for a cornerback is not
sourced to a newspaper but *per Kent Somers of The Arizona Republic* — the
reporter named, the paper attached, in a national outlet with no obligation to
do either. Given room, the reach gets longer. Handed 3,219 words on the Raiders'
last home game in Oakland, he opened on a poem the Oakland Tribune ran in
1907, moved through Gary Oldman researching Lee Harvey Oswald
for Oliver Stone, and used the actor's remark about the subject being better
than a relationship as his figure for a city and a football team — introduced,
left alone for two paragraphs, then picked up and paid off. The team itself he
called a *rough-and-tumble underdog puncher*.

Sports writing has a supplied vocabulary, and it is a good one. It is fast,
unambiguous, and built for a Tuesday afternoon when eleven things have happened
and all of them need three hundred words. It gives you cornerback, wideout,
first-round pick, season, QB, OC, TE. The register is not a failure of
imagination; it is a solution to a real problem. Most people who write about
football write inside it because writing inside it is the job.

What is unusual about Sessler is not that he had another vocabulary. It is that
he kept both, and used the second one in places where the first would have been
easier and no one would have known.

---

You can count this, up to a point. The archive holds 437 of his pieces and
1,438 by the colleagues who sat around him at Around the League — same beat,
same site, same years, mostly the same Tuesday afternoons. Scored against them,
*autumn* is the second most distinctive word in his corpus: 78 of his articles,
4 of theirs. *Cover man* appears in 47 of his and none of theirs at all. *A
flock of* — 53 to zero. *Play caller*, 74 to 14. *First-rounder*, 53 to 4.

The arithmetic is real but it is also, on its own, a little dishonest, and it
took a second pass to see why. Three quarters of his surviving words come from
long columns written after 2019; the colleagues' corpus is overwhelmingly short
transaction items, and holds almost no long pieces at all. Some of what looks
like voice is just room. A 1,500-word column has room to call a defensive line
*a flock of beguiled interior linemen*. A waiver-wire note does not.

So the comparison has to be made narrower before it means anything: his 229
short items from 2012 to 2018 against their 1,315 from the same years — same
form, same length, same desk. Several of the patterns I liked best did not
survive that. The nature words — chaos, starry, earth, eons — turn out to occur
once or twice in 229 briefs; they belong to the long columns, not to him. So
does the martial vocabulary: he uses *war* and *battle* at exactly the rate
everyone else does, because that is the sport's idiom and not anyone's style.
The habit I was most confident about, the little judged sentence he lands at the
end of a paragraph, is simply what the form does — his colleagues do it slightly
more often than he does.

What is left after that is smaller and much harder to argue with.

In those matched short items, he uses the plain words at the ordinary rate:
cornerback, wideout, season, all within a few percent of his colleagues. He is
not avoiding English. But he uses *cover man* twelve times where they use it
zero times in 416,000 words, *play caller* at eleven times their rate,
*first-rounder* at seven times theirs. And he uses the shorthand — QB, TE, OC,
DB — at *less than half* their rate. The pair that gives it away is
first-round pick against first-rounder: same player, same fact, and he takes the
first at a third of their rate and the second at seven times. It is a
substitution, made steadily, in the least-read writing on the internet.

He also names the reporter. Attributions that give the writer and the paper
together run six times more often in his short items than in theirs — the same
twenty instances, in a body of text a sixth the size. It is a small courtesy paid
to beat writers at metro dailies by a man at a national site who could have
written "per reports" and gone home.

None of this is what the counting was built to find. n-grams see words and short
phrases; they cannot see a metaphor deferred for two paragraphs, or the fact
that in *the once-physical and reliable cover man* the borrowed register sits in
the load-bearing clause rather than being spread over the top as decoration.
That part is reading, and reading is interpretation. The numbers say the words
are his. What the words are doing is my argument, and you are entitled to a
different one.

---

Most of it went off the internet, and nothing dramatic happened.

Around 2016 NFL.com changed content systems and the URL scheme changed with it.
The old addresses — the ones with the long numeric content IDs, the ones every
link and every search result pointed at — stopped resolving. This is not a
story about suppression. Nobody made a decision about Marc Sessler's prose.
Somebody made a decision about a database, correctly, on a deadline, and the
consequence was invisible from inside the building because the new site worked
fine. The writing did not get taken down. It got un-addressed. It is the
ordinary way that things on the internet stop existing: not deletion but the
quiet expiry of the pointer, and nobody whose job it was to care.

What was recoverable came back out of the Internet Archive, one capture at a
time — 1,903 articles in the end, with all the seams that implies. The years
2014 to 2018 nearly went missing a second time: the obvious starting points, the
author page and the section front, reached six articles from that whole span.
Everything else was sitting on numbered pagination pages that no sensible guess
would have found, and one wildcard query turned six into 1,311. Two-thirds of
the archive was one query away from staying lost, which is a useful thing to
know about how confident anyone should be that a thing is gone.

Five articles are gone for good. They were linked from pages we hold, so we know
they were published; no capture of them exists anywhere, and four of the five
are from the same stretch of the 2012 preseason — roster cutdowns, the most
disposable week in the calendar. Somewhere in those five is a sentence about a
player nobody remembers, and it is not coming back. Against 1,908 known pieces
that is a rounding error, and it is also the only part of this that is actually
permanent.

The colleagues are not a foil in any of this. They were good at a hard job, and
one of them, Chris Wesseling, died while the show they made together was still
going out. His 164 pieces are in the same archive, holding up the other side of
the comparison, which is a strange thing for a body of work to be asked to do
and worth saying out loud.

There is a poem Steve Sabol wrote for NFL Films about the Raiders, decades ago —
the league's own one moment of reaching for a literary register and getting away
with it. It is remembered because it was on television. The point of this
archive is that the same reach was happening every week, in 149-word items about
tryouts, where it could not be seen and was not rewarded and stopped resolving
in 2016, and that the reaching is legible now only because somebody went and
counted.

He kept finding better words than the job required, and the job was never going
to notice. Four hundred and thirty-seven files of it are back.
