# Words for the Autumn Wind

On 11 July 2018, NFL.com published 149 words about a tryout. The Cleveland
Browns had spent a day looking at a defensive back from the Canadian league, a
23-year-old who had washed through the Edmonton Eskimos and an arena team in
Ohio and would never play a down in the NFL. The item exists because a team
worked out a player and somebody has to write that down. It is the lowest form
in the trade — no argument, no access, no reader who is not already lost.

The last fact in it calls him:

::quote a 5-foot-10, 190-pound cover man | Browns try out CFL defensive back Jordan Holland | 2018-07-11

Not a defensive back. The headline says defensive back. Eight other pieces went
up on that site the same day, and one of them covered the same event — that
week's supplemental draft — and the same position: it wrote CB six times and
corner six times. Across the whole of that July his colleagues named a defensive
back twenty times in fifteen thousand words, nine of those as CB or DB. Not one
of them wrote cover man. But in the sentence carrying the only original detail
in the piece — the height, the weight, the undrafted college — the writer
reached past the available word and took a different one. Nobody was going to
notice. That, I think, is the whole point.

The writer was Marc Sessler, and he did this for more than a decade. A backup
quarterback in a March roster item will probably not survive to September; he is

::quote looking for work come autumn | Cowboys' Stephen Jones expects Kyle Orton back | 2014-03-24

Given room, the reach gets longer. Handed 3,219 words on the Raiders' last home
game in Oakland, he opened on a poem the Oakland Tribune ran in 1907, moved
through Gary Oldman researching Lee Harvey Oswald for Oliver Stone, and used the
actor's remark about the subject being better than a relationship as his figure
for a city and a football team — introduced, left alone for two paragraphs, then
picked up and paid off. The team itself he called a

::quote rough-and-tumble underdog puncher | Final home game in Oakland bittersweet farewell for Raiders | 2019-12-10

Sports writing has a supplied vocabulary, and it is a good one: fast,
unambiguous, built for a Tuesday when eleven things have happened and all of
them need three hundred words. It gives you cornerback, wideout, first-round
pick, season, QB, OC, TE. That register is not a failure of imagination. It is a
solution to a real problem, and most people who write about football write
inside it because writing inside it is the job.

---

Here is the strongest single thing the counting found.

```figure:substitution```

Same player, same fact, the same number of syllables. He takes the newsroom's
phrase at a third of their rate and his own at seven times theirs, in the
least-read writing on the internet, for six years.

That is one pair out of a comparison between his 437 recovered pieces and 1,438
by the colleagues who sat around him at Around the League — same beat, same
site, mostly the same Tuesday afternoons.

The comparison needed narrowing before it meant anything. Three quarters of his
surviving words are long columns written after 2019, and the colleagues' corpus
is almost entirely short transaction items with barely a long piece in it. Some
of what looked like voice was just room: a 1,500-word column has space to call a
defensive line

::quote a flock of beguiled interior linemen | 2021 NFL season: One potential first-time Pro Bowler from each NFC team | 2021-07-08

and a waiver-wire note does not. So the real test is his 229 short items from
2012 to 2018 against their 1,315 from the same years — same form, same length,
same desk. Several of the patterns I liked best did not survive it.

```figure:dead```

What is left is smaller and much harder to argue with. In those matched short
items he reaches for the plain words about as often as anyone does: season and
wideout within a few percent of his colleagues, cornerback slightly more often
than they manage it. He is not avoiding English. He is running a second vocabulary alongside the first, and declining the
newsroom's shorthand while he does it: position abbreviations turn up in his
briefs at under half the rate they do in theirs.

::note cover man | 12 | 0
::note play caller | 17 | 9
::note passer | 54 | 69
::note under center | 22 | 35

Take autumn. In those matched briefs it appears six times in his 68,000 words
and not once in their 417,000 — a small count on his side, a true zero on
theirs. Across everything he wrote it is the second most distinctive word in his
corpus, 78 of his articles against 4 of theirs, but that larger figure is the
contaminated one: it counts his columns against their briefs. The honest number
is six and nothing.

He also names the reporter. Attributions that give the writer and the paper
together run more than five times as often in his short items as in theirs —
twenty instances of his against twenty-two of theirs, in a sixth of the text. A
small courtesy paid to beat writers at metro dailies by a man at a national site who could have
written "per reports" and gone home.

::quote per Kent Somers of The Arizona Republic | Arians: Justin Bethel had 'best spring of anybody' | 2014-07-24

None of this is what the counting was built to find. n-grams see words and short
phrases; they cannot see a metaphor deferred for two paragraphs, or the fact
that in

::quote the once-physical and reliable cover man | Cortland Finnegan will be released by St. Louis Rams | 2014-03-05

the borrowed register sits in the load-bearing clause rather than spread over
the top as decoration. That part is reading, and reading is interpretation. The
numbers say the words are his. What the words are doing is my argument, and you
are entitled to a different one.

---

Most of it went off the internet, and nothing dramatic happened.

Around 2016 NFL.com changed content systems and the URL scheme changed with it.
The old addresses — the ones with the long numeric content IDs, the ones every
link and every search result pointed at — stopped resolving. Nobody made a
decision about Marc Sessler's prose. Somebody made a decision about a database,
correctly, on a deadline, and the consequence was invisible from inside the
building because the new site worked fine. The writing was not taken down. It
was un-addressed: not deletion but the quiet expiry of the pointer, and nobody
whose job it was to care.

```figure:years```

What was recoverable came back out of the Internet Archive one capture at a
time. The years 2014 to 2018 nearly went missing a second time — the obvious
starting points, the author page and the section front, reached six articles
from that whole span. Everything else sat on numbered pagination pages that no
sensible guess would have found, and one wildcard query turned six into 1,311.
Two-thirds of the archive was one query away from staying lost, which is worth
knowing before anyone declares a thing gone.

Five articles are gone for good. They were linked from pages we hold, so we know
they were published; no capture of them exists anywhere, and four of the five
are from the same stretch of the 2012 preseason — roster cutdowns, the most
disposable week in the calendar. Against 1,908 known pieces that is a rounding
error, and it is also the only part of this that is permanent.

The colleagues are not a foil in any of this. They were good at a hard job, and
one of them, Chris Wesseling, died while the show they made together was still
going out. His 164 pieces are in the same archive, holding up the other side of
the comparison, which is a strange thing to ask of a body of work and worth
saying out loud.

There is a poem Steve Sabol wrote for NFL Films about the Raiders, decades ago —
the league's own one moment of reaching for a literary register and getting away
with it. It is remembered because it was on television. The point of this
archive is that the same reach was happening every week, in 149-word items about
tryouts, where it could not be seen and was not rewarded and stopped resolving
in 2016.

He kept finding better words than the job required, and the job was never going
to notice. Four hundred and thirty-seven files of it are back.
